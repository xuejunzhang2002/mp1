About the bear

Meet the sea bear

Its scientific name, Ursus maritimus, means “sea bear”, and it fits. Polar bears spend most of their lives on the frozen surface of the Arctic Ocean, and they are the only bears classified as marine mammals.

The Inuit call it nanuq. Around 26,000 polar bears live across the Arctic in 19 subpopulations, spread over Canada, the United States, Russia, Greenland and Norway.

  • Built for the cold

    Under the fur the skin is black, which soaks up heat. The outer hairs are actually transparent and hollow, and a layer of fat about 10 cm thick keeps the cold out.

  • Largest land carnivore

    Adult males usually weigh 350–700 kg, around twice as much as females. Standing on their hind legs, the biggest males are close to 3 metres tall.

  • Snowshoe paws

    Paws up to 30 cm across spread their weight on thin ice and work as paddles in the water. Small bumps on the pads help them grip without slipping.

Field note 01

Still-hunting at the breathing hole

Ringed and bearded seals make up most of a polar bear's diet. Seals keep breathing holes open in the ice, and a bear will stand or lie completely still beside one for hours, waiting for a seal to come up for air. This is called still-hunting.

A strong sense of smell lets bears find seals from about a kilometre away, even under a layer of snow. They eat the calorie-rich blubber first. Spring on the sea ice is when they build up most of the fat they need for the rest of the year.

  • 2 speciesMain prey: ringed and bearded seals
  • ~1 kmDistance a bear can smell a seal from
  • SpringPeak hunting season on the ice

Field note 02

Swimmers of the open sea

Polar bears are strong swimmers. They paddle with their big front paws and steer with their hind legs, and they regularly swim between ice floes or from the ice to the shore.

As the ice pulls back, those swims are getting longer. In 2008 scientists tracked a female in the Beaufort Sea who swam for nine days straight and covered 687 km. She survived, but lost 22% of her body weight, and the yearling cub swimming with her did not make it.

  • 687 kmLongest continuous swim on record
  • 9 daysHow long that swim lasted
  • 22%Body weight she lost on the way

Field note 03

Life in a snow den

In autumn, pregnant females dig maternity dens in deep snowdrifts. The cubs, usually twins, are born in mid-winter. They are born blind and weigh only about 600 g.

The family stays in the den until March or April, and the mother eats nothing the whole time. Counting the months before she denned, she can go up to eight months without food. After they leave the den, the cubs stay with her for about two and a half years.

  • ~600 gWeight of a newborn cub
  • 8 monthsLongest fast for a denning mother
  • 2.5 yearsTime cubs spend with their mother

Get involved

How you can help

The biggest threat to polar bears is the loss of the sea ice they hunt from. Here is where to start.

  • Cut your carbon

    Sea-ice loss is driven by a warming climate. Using less energy, travelling smarter and backing clean power all help keep the ice frozen for longer.

  • Back the science

    Long-term monitoring of all 19 subpopulations shows where bears are struggling. Research and conservation groups depend on public support.

  • Spread the word

    International Polar Bear Day is 27 February. Share what you have learned, because what happens to the ice decides what happens to the bear.
